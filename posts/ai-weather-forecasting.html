When Machines Started Beating the Weatherman

Weather forecasting is one of the oldest, most physics-heavy problems computers have ever tackled. Neural networks are now doing it better, without solving a single equation of motion.

Dev Shree Saini· November 2025· 6 min read

Operational weather forecasting has run on the same basic idea for seventy years. Take the current state of the atmosphere, and numerically integrate the physical equations that govern how fluids and heat move, forward in time, on a supercomputer. It works. It's why forecasts are dramatically better than they were a generation ago, and centres like the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) have spent decades refining it into the closest thing the field has to a gold standard. So it's a genuinely strange moment when a model that never solves those equations starts beating it.

01The old way: simulate the atmosphere

Traditional numerical weather prediction (NWP) discretises the atmosphere into a 3D grid and solves the physical equations of fluid motion and thermodynamics at every grid point, every few minutes of simulated time, for days of forecast horizon. It's an enormous, physically principled computation, and it needs a dedicated supercomputer cluster running for hours to produce a single global forecast.

02The new way: learn the pattern, skip the equations

GraphCast, released by Google DeepMind, takes a completely different approach. Instead of simulating physics, it's a graph neural network trained on 39 years of historical reanalysis data (ERA5) to learn, directly from examples, what tends to happen next given the current state of the atmosphere. No equations of motion get solved at inference time at all. It's closer to pattern completion than simulation. And yet, tested across more than a thousand variable and lead-time combinations out to 10 days, GraphCast outperformed ECMWF's high-resolution deterministic forecast (HRES) on over 90% of them, running a full 10-day global forecast in under a minute on a single TPU.

03GenCast: doing it with uncertainty, not just a single guess

A single deterministic forecast was never the full picture. Operational forecasting relies on ensembles, dozens of slightly perturbed simulations run in parallel, because the atmosphere is chaotic and a single trajectory can't capture that. ECMWF's ensemble system, ENS, is the benchmark here. GenCast, DeepMind's diffusion-based follow-up to GraphCast, generates an ensemble of forecasts the same way image diffusion models generate pictures, starting from noise and iteratively refining it, and was tested against ENS on 1,320 targets. It won on 97.2% of them, a figure that climbed to 99.8% at lead times beyond 36 hours. It also produces a full 15-day global forecast in about eight minutes on a single TPU v5 chip, next to a conventional run that needs hours on a dedicated cluster consuming megawatts.

04Why this is stranger than it sounds

Weather is governed by well-understood physics, the Navier–Stokes equations, thermodynamics, radiative transfer. It's not a domain where you'd expect a model with no explicit physical knowledge built in to out-forecast the people who spent decades encoding exactly that physics into a simulator. The resolution to the paradox isn't that physics stopped mattering. It's that the historical reanalysis data these models train on was itself generated by assimilating decades of real observations into physical models, so the physics is baked into the training signal even though it's never solved at inference time. These systems aren't a replacement for physical understanding of the atmosphere, they're a very efficient way of reusing it. ECMWF has already made its own AI model, AIFS, fully operational since February 2025, running it side by side with the traditional physics-based IFS rather than treating machine learning as a rival to be beaten.

05What's not solved yet

Extreme, rare events are under-represented in any historical training set almost by definition, which is exactly the case where a forecast being wrong matters most. Local, fine-grained forecasting, the kind that matters for a specific valley or a specific storm cell, still leans heavily on physical models at finer resolution than these global systems currently run at. There's also a quieter dependency worth noting. These models are trained on reanalysis data produced by traditional physical simulation in the first place, so the two approaches are still more entangled than the "AI beats physics" headlines suggest.

The short version. A model that has never once solved an equation of atmospheric motion is now beating the forecasting system built on exactly those equations, on most of the metrics that matter, in a fraction of the time and compute. That's not a small result. It's a genuine shift in how one of the oldest applied physics problems in computing gets solved.

I don't work in atmospheric science, but this sits close enough to my own interest in what neural networks can absorb from physically grounded data that I keep coming back to it.
